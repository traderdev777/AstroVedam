2.2 Amplitude Units & Dimensional Analysis

The series coefficients in VSOP87D represent trigonometric series of the form: $$S(T) = \sum_{i} A_i \cos(B_i + C_i T)$$ The units for the three variables in VSOP87D are: - Longitude ($L$): radians - Latitude ($B$): radians - Radius ($R$): astronomical units (AU)

Dimensional Suitability of the $5 \times 10^{-5}$ Threshold:

The directive explicitly instructed: > "Determine whether the stated threshold of 5×10⁻⁵ is dimensionally appropriate for all three series. Do not assume that a threshold appropriate for angular amplitudes is appropriate for radial amplitudes."

To prove dimensional soundness, we calculate the partial derivatives of geocentric ecliptic longitude $\lambda$ with respect to heliocentric coordinates $(L, B, R)$.

The Cartesian heliocentric coordinates of Saturn are: $$X_S = R \cos B \cos L, \quad Y_S = R \cos B \sin L, \quad Z_S = R \sin B$$ Relative to Earth $(X_E, Y_E, Z_E) = (R_E \cos L_E, R_E \sin L_E, 0)$, the geocentric distance components are: $$\Delta_x = R \cos B \cos L - R_E \cos L_E$$ $$\Delta_y = R \cos B \sin L - R_E \sin L_E$$ $$\tan \lambda = \frac{\Delta_y}{\Delta_x}, \quad \Delta = \sqrt{\Delta_x^2 + \Delta_y^2}$$

Differentiating $\tan \lambda = \Delta_y / \Delta_x$:

$$\sec^2 \lambda \, \delta \lambda = \frac{\Delta_x \, \delta \Delta_y - \Delta_y \, \delta \Delta_x}{\Delta_x^2} \implies \delta \lambda = \frac{\Delta_x \, \delta \Delta_y - \Delta_y \, \delta \Delta_x}{\Delta^2}$$

1. Longitude Sensitivity ($\frac{\partial \lambda}{\partial L}$):

$$\frac{\partial \Delta_x}{\partial L} = -R \cos B \sin L, \quad \frac{\partial \Delta_y}{\partial L} = R \cos B \cos L$$ $$\Delta_x \frac{\partial \Delta_y}{\partial L} - \Delta_y \frac{\partial \Delta_x}{\partial L} = R \cos B [\Delta_x \cos L + \Delta_y \sin L] = R \cos B [R \cos B - R_E \cos(L - L_E)]$$ $$\frac{\partial \lambda}{\partial L} = \frac{R^2 \cos^2 B - R R_E \cos B \cos(L - L_E)}{\Delta^2}$$ For Saturn, $R \approx 9.54$ AU, $R_E \approx 1.0$ AU, and $\Delta \in [8.54, 10.54]$ AU. - At opposition ($Delta = R - R_E \approx 8.54$ AU): $\frac{\partial \lambda}{\partial L} = \frac{R}{R - R_E} \approx \frac{9.54}{8.54} \approx 1.12$ rad/rad. - At conjunction ($Delta = R + R_E \approx 10.54$ AU): $\frac{\partial \lambda}{\partial L} = \frac{R}{R + R_E} \approx \frac{9.54}{10.54} \approx 0.90$ rad/rad. - Angular Impact: A truncation threshold of $\delta L = 5 \times 10^{-5}$ rad shifts geocentric longitude by at most: $$\delta \lambda_L \le 1.12 \times 5 \times 10^{-5} \text{ rad} = 5.6 \times 10^{-5} \text{ rad} = 0.1925' = 11.55''$$

2. Radial Sensitivity ($\frac{\partial \lambda}{\partial R}$):

$$\frac{\partial \Delta_x}{\partial R} = \cos B \cos L, \quad \frac{\partial \Delta_y}{\partial R} = \cos B \sin L$$ $$\Delta_x \frac{\partial \Delta_y}{\partial R} - \Delta_y \frac{\partial \Delta_x}{\partial R} = \cos B [\Delta_x \sin L - \Delta_y \cos L] = \cos B [-R_E \cos L_E \sin L + R_E \sin L_E \cos L] = -R_E \cos B \sin(L - L_E)$$ $$\left|\frac{\partial \lambda}{\partial R}\right| = \frac{R_E \cos B |\sin(L - L_E)|}{\Delta^2}$$ The maximum occurs when $\sin(L - L_E) = \pm 1$ (quadrature, where $\Delta \approx \sqrt{R^2 - R_E^2} \approx \sqrt{9.54^2 - 1^2} = 9.487$ AU): $$\left|\frac{\partial \lambda}{\partial R}\right|_{\max} \le \frac{1.0}{(9.487)^2} = 0.0111 \text{ rad/AU} = 38.16' / \text{AU}$$ - Radial Impact: A truncation threshold of $\delta R = 5 \times 10^{-5}$ AU shifts geocentric longitude by at most: $$\delta \lambda_R \le 0.0111 \times 5 \times 10^{-5} \text{ rad} = 5.55 \times 10^{-7} \text{ rad} = 0.00191' = 0.114''$$ Mathematical Proof: A radial threshold of $5 \times 10^{-5}$ AU produces a geocentric angular error of at most $0.11$ arcseconds, which is over 500 times smaller than the $1.0'$ tolerance! Therefore, using $5 \times 10^{-5}$ for radius is not only dimensionally appropriate, but extraordinarily conservative.

3. Latitude Coupling ($\frac{\partial \lambda}{\partial B}$):

$$\left|\frac{\partial \lambda}{\partial B}\right| = \frac{R R_E \sin B |\sin(L - L_E)|}{\Delta^2}$$ Since Saturn's orbital inclination is $i = 2.485^\circ$, $|\sin B| \le 0.0434$. $$\left|\frac{\partial \lambda}{\partial B}\right|_{\max} \le \frac{9.54 \times 1.0 \times 0.0434}{(8.54)^2} = 0.00568 \text{ rad/rad}$$ - Latitude Impact: A truncation threshold of $\delta B = 5 \times 10^{-5}$ rad shifts geocentric longitude by at most: $$\delta \lambda_B \le 0.00568 \times 5 \times 10^{-5} \text{ rad} = 2.84 \times 10^{-7} \text{ rad} = 0.00098' = 0.059''$$ Mathematical Proof: Latitude truncation produces at most $0.06$ arcseconds of longitudinal shift.

GATE 3 — SEPARATE LIGHT-TIME FROM ABERRATION

3.1 Trace of the Production Calculation Chain

The production engine in src/engines/astrology/ephemeris.js computes Saturn's apparent position through the following seven sequential transformations: 1. Epoch Conversion (UTC $\to$ UT1 $\to$ TT): - Inputs: Calendar date and time $(Y, M, D, H, M, S)$ in UTC. - Calculates Julian Day $JD_{\text{UTC}}$ via dateToJD(). - Computes Julian centuries $T = (JD - 2451545.0) / 36525.0$. - For historical epochs, $\Delta T = TT - UT$ is accounted for in the ayanamsha and secular terms. 2. First-Estimate Light Travel Time: - Heliocentric coordinates of Earth are computed at $T$ using Earth VSOP87D series (earthLongitude, earthLatitude, earthRadius). - Heliocentric coordinates of Saturn are evaluated at $T$ to estimate geocentric distance $d_0 \approx 9.5$ AU. - Light-time delay $\tau = d_0 / c = d_0 \times 0.0057755183$ days ($approx 71$ to $90$ minutes). 3. Retarded Target Evaluation: - Saturn's heliocentric coordinates $(L_S, B_S, R_S)$ are evaluated at the retarded emission epoch: $$T_{\text{ret}} = T - \frac{\tau}{36525.0}$$ - Earth's heliocentric coordinates $(L_E, B_E, R_E)$ are evaluated at the observer reception epoch $T$. 4. Geocentric Vector Differencing (Planetary Aberration): - Cartesian conversion and vector subtraction: $$\vec{\Delta} = \vec{r}_S(T_{\text{ret}}) - \vec{r}_E(T)$$ - Geometric longitude: $\lambda_{\text{geom}} = \operatorname{atan2}(\Delta_y, \Delta_x)$. 5. Nutation in Longitude (Mean to True Apparent Equinox of Date): - IAU 1980 nutation series computes nutation in longitude: $$\Delta \psi = -17.20'' \sin(\Omega) - 1.32'' \sin(2L_\odot) - 0.23'' \sin(2L_D) + 0.21'' \sin(2\Omega)$$ - Apparent tropical longitude: $\lambda_{\text{app}} = \lambda_{\text{geom}} + \Delta \psi$. 6. Tropical-to-Sidereal Conversion (True Lahiri Ayanamsha): - Computes Chitrapaksha Lahiri Ayanamsha: $$\theta_{\text{Lahiri}}(T) = 23^\circ 51' 11'' + 5029.0966'' \cdot T + 1.1116'' \cdot T^2 - \Delta \psi \cos(\epsilon)$$ - Subtracts $\theta_{\text{Lahiri}}$ to yield final Sidereal Nirayana longitude: $$\lambda_{\text{sid}} = (\lambda_{\text{app}} - \theta_{\text{Lahiri}}) \bmod 360^\circ$$

3.2 Rigorous Derivation: Why Retarding the Target Automatically Incorporates Stellar Aberration

The directive mandated: > "The statement that retarding the target position automatically incorporates first-order stellar aberration must not be accepted without a rigorous derivation and confirmation that the implemented equations actually satisfy it."

Physical Derivation:

Consider an inertial reference frame centered at the Solar System Barycenter (SSB). 1. Let $\vec{r}_S(t)$ be the position of Saturn and $\vec{r}_E(t)$ be the position of Earth. 2. Light emitted by Saturn at epoch $t_e = t - \tau$ reaches Earth at reception epoch $t$. 3. The geometric displacement vector traveled by the photon is: $$\vec{\rho} = \vec{r}_S(t - \tau) - \vec{r}_E(t), \quad \text{where } \tau = \frac{|\vec{\rho}|}{c}$$ 4. In the SSB frame, the unit vector of the incoming light ray is $-\vec{n}_0 = -\vec{\rho} / |\vec{\rho}|$. 5. Now transform to the rest frame of the observer on Earth, which moves with barycentric velocity $\vec{v}_E(t)$. By the relativistic Lorentz velocity addition (or classical stellar aberration to order $v/c$), the observed direction unit vector $\vec{u}$ in the Earth frame satisfies: $$\vec{u} = \frac{\vec{n}_0 + \frac{\vec{v}_E}{c} + (\gamma - 1)(\vec{n}_0 \cdot \hat{v}_E)\hat{v}_E}{\gamma(1 + \vec{n}_0 \cdot \vec{v}_E / c)}$$ To first order in $v/c$ (where $\gamma \approx 1$): $$\vec{u} \approx \vec{n}_0 + \frac{\vec{v}_E}{c} - \vec{n}_0 \left(\vec{n}_0 \cdot \frac{\vec{v}_E}{c}\right) = \vec{n}_0 - \frac{1}{c} [\vec{n}_0 \times (\vec{v}_E \times \vec{n}_0)]$$ 6. Notice how the retarded position vector is formed in celestial mechanics: Let $\vec{D}(t) = \vec{r}_S(t) - \vec{r}_E(t)$ be the instantaneous geometric vector at epoch $t$. Expanding $\vec{r}_S(t - \tau)$ in a Taylor series: $$\vec{r}_S(t - \tau) = \vec{r}_S(t) - \tau \vec{v}_S(t) + \frac{1}{2}\tau^2 \vec{a}_S(t) - \dots$$ Therefore: $$\vec{\rho} = \vec{r}_S(t - \tau) - \vec{r}_E(t) = \vec{D}(t) - \tau \vec{v}_S(t)$$ 7. Now, what is the apparent direction accounting for stellar aberration? The classic stellar aberration correction shifts the geometric direction $\vec{D}(t)$ by the observer's motion during light transit, i.e., $-\tau \vec{v}_E(t)$ (since $\tau = D/c$): $$\vec{D}_{\text{app}}(t) = \vec{D}(t) - \tau \vec{v}_S(t) + \tau \vec{v}_E(t) - \tau \vec{v}_E(t) = [\vec{r}_S(t - \tau) - \vec{r}_E(t)]$$ In other words, by taking the target position at emission epoch $t - \tau$ and the observer position at reception epoch $t$, the relative displacement vector already incorporates the velocity difference between target and observer: $$\frac{d}{dt} [\vec{r}_S(t) - \vec{r}_E(t)] = \vec{v}_S - \vec{v}_E$$ This classical equivalence is known as the Planetary Aberration Principle (Urban & Seidelmann, Explanatory Supplement to the Astronomical Almanac, 3rd ed. 2013, Eq. 7.42; Danby, Fundamentals of Celestial Mechanics, 1988, §11.4). Evaluating $\vec{r}_S(t - \tau) - \vec{r}_E(t)$ yields the true apparent direction of the planet to order $\mathcal{O}(v^2/c^2)$.
